A short history of the motorcycle

Every part on a modern motorcycle answers a problem someone met along the way: an engine that could not idle, a front wheel that slid, exhaust that broke the emission rules. This timeline follows those problems from the steam velocipedes of 1868 to battery swapping.

From steam velocipede to battery swapping

Six periods, each with the dates and machines that changed how motorcycles are built. Dates and facts come from chapter 1.1 of Module 1 and from the history notes in Modules 03 to 05.

1867 to 1888

Four inventions meet

A practical motorcycle needed a compact engine, the pneumatic tyre, the roller chain and a frame able to carry a rider and a power unit. All four arrived within about twenty years.

  1. 1867–69

    Roper’s steam velocipede

    Sylvester Roper builds a steam-powered two-wheeler in the United States, one of the first powered machines of its kind.

  2. 1868

    Perreaux patents a small steam engine

    Louis Guillaume Perreaux files his patent on 26 December 1868. By 1871 the engine is fitted to a Michaux iron-framed velocipede, with a pulley drive to the rear wheel.

  3. 1876

    Otto runs a four-stroke engine

    Nikolaus Otto runs a four-stroke engine: intake, compression, power, exhaust. Most combustion motorcycles still use this cycle.

  4. 1880

    The bush roller chain

    Renold’s bush roller chain gives a compact way to take drive to the rear wheel.

  5. 1885

    The Daimler Reitwagen

    Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach mount a small high-speed petrol engine in a wooden frame with outrigger wheels. It is an engine test bed, not a product.

  6. 1886

    The Starley safety bicycle

    Two wheels of similar size, a chain to the rear wheel and a diamond-shaped frame: the geometry the first motorcycles borrowed.

  7. 1888 and 1895

    The pneumatic tyre

    Dunlop’s pneumatic tyre of 1888, followed by Michelin’s in 1895, completes the set of four inventions.

1894 to 1903

The first motorcycles

The first machines sold to the public showed what worked and what did not. Within ten years the basic layout was settled.

  1. 1894

    Hildebrand & Wolfmüller

    The first machine sold in series and called a “motorrad”: a water-cooled 1,489 cc twin whose connecting rods drove cranks on the rear wheel directly, with no flywheel or gearbox. About one thousand were built before the company failed in 1897.

  2. 1897

    The Werner

    The Werner carries its engine above the front wheel. The high centre of mass makes the front wheel prone to slide.

  3. 1901

    The New Werner and Indian

    The New Werner moves the engine down to the bottom-bracket position between the wheels. In the same year Indian is founded in the United States.

  4. 1903

    The layout is settled; Harley-Davidson

    By about 1903 the engine low and central with the tank above it is established practice. Harley-Davidson is founded; with Indian it is one of the two American makers to survive the 1930s.

What the first machines taught

The Hildebrand & Wolfmüller proved there was demand, but a direct drive with no flywheel could neither idle nor pull away cleanly. The Werner showed that engine position decides how a motorcycle handles. Both lessons are still visible on every machine in the workshop.

1912 to 1950

Two-strokes, twins and better forks

Between the wars motorcycles gained overhead valves, chain final drive and the engine layouts that would be copied for decades.

  1. 1912 and 1913

    Scott two-strokes win the Senior TT

    Alfred Scott’s water-cooled two-stroke twins win the Isle of Man Senior TT in two successive years.

  2. 1920s–30s

    Overhead valves and chain final drive

    The interwar years bring overhead valves and chain final drive into general use.

  3. 1937

    The Triumph Speed Twin

    Edward Turner’s Speed Twin sets the parallel-twin layout that other makers copy for half a century.

  4. After 1945

    The DKW RT125 is copied worldwide

    DKW’s loop-scavenged RT125 is copied as the BSA Bantam, the Harley-Davidson Hummer and Yamaha’s first product, the YA-1. It becomes the template for cheap motorised transport.

  5. Late 1940s

    The telescopic fork

    Just after the Second World War the hydraulically damped telescopic fork becomes general.

1958 to 1975

Mass production

From the late 1950s Japanese mass production changed the economics of the motorcycle, and two-stroke engines reached very high outputs.

  1. 1958

    The Honda Super Cub

    A step-through with a 49 cc overhead-valve four-stroke and an automatic centrifugal clutch. It is generally described as the most-produced motor vehicle in history.

  2. 1969

    The Honda CB750

    An overhead-camshaft inline-four and a front disc brake reach the mass market.

  3. 1960s–70s

    Two-stroke development

    Suzuki, Kawasaki and Yamaha add reed valves, exhaust power valves and expansion chambers. Specific outputs rise sharply, but a ported two-stroke loses part of its fresh charge into the exhaust and burns its lubricating oil.

  4. 1975

    Linked brakes

    Moto Guzzi fits linked “integral” brakes on the 850 T3, an early form of the combined braking later required on small motorcycles.

1970s to 2020

Rules shape the machine

Emission limits and braking rules, more than fashion, decided what a road motorcycle looks like today: fuel injection, a catalytic converter and ABS or CBS.

  1. Late 1970s

    US emission rules

    US EPA rules begin the pressure on two-stroke road machines, whose hydrocarbon emissions are many times those of a four-stroke.

  2. 1980

    Toothed belt drive

    Harley-Davidson starts using toothed belts with carbon or aramid cords for final drive. They are now common on electric motorcycles.

  3. 1988

    The first production ABS

    BMW offers the first production motorcycle ABS on the K100.

  4. 2005–10

    Euro 3 and Bharat Stage II and III

    Euro 3 (2006) in Europe and Bharat Stage II and III in India push road two-strokes out of most markets. They survive in off-road competition, some direct-injection designs and older fleets.

  5. 2013

    Stability control at lean

    Bosch Motorcycle Stability Control is first fitted on the KTM 1190 Adventure.

  6. 2016–17

    Euro 4 and compulsory ABS in the EU

    Euro 4 applies from 2016. Under Regulation (EU) No 168/2013 ABS becomes compulsory above 125 cc (new types from 2016, all new registrations from 2017), with ABS or CBS on the 125 cc class.

  7. 2017

    One hundred million Super Cubs

    The Super Cub passes 100 million units in October 2017.

  8. 2018–19

    ABS and CBS in India

    India requires ABS above 125 cc and CBS at or below 125 cc on new models from April 2018 and on all models from April 2019.

  9. 2020

    Euro 5 and BS-VI: the end of the carburettor

    Euro 5 (new types from 1 January 2020) and India’s BS-VI (1 April 2020) require the mixture to be held at stoichiometric, 14.7:1 for petrol, so a three-way catalyst can work. In practice that means fuel injection with an oxygen sensor, even on 100–125 cc commuters such as the K-Light 125.

2015 to today

The electric transition

The current transition is electric, and it is happening fastest where small motorcycles and scooters carry most of the traffic.

  1. 2015

    Gogoro battery swapping

    Gogoro introduces its swap network in Taiwan. Automated stations release a charged pack in seconds; by 2020 Gogoro operates about 1,700 sites in Taiwan.

  2. 2019

    A common Japanese battery

    Honda, Kawasaki, Suzuki and Yamaha form the Swappable Batteries Motorcycle Consortium to agree common specifications for swappable batteries and stations.

  3. 2022

    Gachaco

    The four makers and the energy company ENEOS create Gachaco, a swap service in Tokyo using a shared pack of about 1.3 kWh.

  4. 2020s

    China and India

    China’s fleet of electric two-wheelers is commonly estimated at more than 300 million. India’s electric two-wheeler sales pass one million a year in the mid-2020s.

  5. 2020s

    East Africa

    Where motorcycle taxis (boda bodas in Kenya and Uganda, motos in Rwanda) carry a large share of urban trips, firms such as Roam, Spiro and Ampersand build or import electric motorcycles and run swapping networks. Spiro reports over 50,000 machines and over 1,000 swap stations in late 2025.

Where this history comes from

The early part of this history draws on Charles M. Falco’s essay on the technical evolution of the motorcycle in the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum’s exhibition catalogue The Art of the Motorcycle (New York, 1998). Falco sets out the four inventions that had to meet and records the production figures for the Hildebrand & Wolfmüller. The regulatory dates come from the regulations cited in Modules 03 and 04, and the electric figures from Module 5.
