## Primary drive

The crankshaft of a 125 cc single turns at 1,500 rpm at idle and up to about 10,000 rpm at full power, while the rear wheel of a motorcycle at 90 km/h turns at roughly 800 rpm. The total speed reduction of about 9:1 to 12:1 in top gear (and 25:1 to 30:1 in first) is split across three stages: the primary drive between crankshaft and clutch, the gearbox, and the final drive to the rear wheel. Splitting the reduction keeps each stage within a sensible size. Putting the first reduction ahead of the clutch means the clutch and gearbox mainshaft spin at only a quarter to a third of crankshaft speed, which reduces the centrifugal load on the clutch basket, the churning losses of the plates in oil, and the inertia that must be synchronised during a gear change.

Most singles and small twins use a **gear primary**: a small pinion on the right-hand end of the crankshaft meshes with a large gear riveted to the back of the clutch basket. Straight-cut (spur) teeth are cheap, carry no axial thrust and are used on most commuter engines; helical teeth run quieter but push the basket sideways, so they need a thrust face or bearing. Because the crank pinion and the basket gear are permanently meshed, any backlash produces a rattle at idle; some engines use a split "scissors" gear in which a spring-loaded half takes up the backlash. Large multi-cylinder engines with long crank-to-mainshaft centres often use a **chain primary**, either a duplex roller chain or an inverted-tooth (Hy-Vo) chain, with a tensioner; older British twins ran a duplex primary chain in an oil bath.

Between the primary gear and the clutch basket there is almost always a set of **cushion springs** (four to six short coil springs or rubber blocks in windows in the gear web). They let the gear rotate a few degrees relative to the basket, absorbing the torque pulses of a single-cylinder firing stroke and the shock of a snatched gear change. Worn cushion springs show as radial play between gear and basket and as a clonk when the throttle is opened and closed in gear.

```eq
i_p = z_basket / z_crank
n_clutch = n_crank / i_p
```

where i_p is the primary ratio, z the number of teeth and n the speed in rpm. A crank pinion of 20 teeth driving a 68-tooth basket gear gives i_p = 68/20 = 3.40, so at 9,000 rpm the clutch turns at 2,647 rpm. Torque rises in the same proportion (less about 1–2 % gear loss): the K-Light 125 peak of 8.9 N·m at 7,500 rpm becomes about 30 N·m at the clutch.

## The wet multiplate clutch

The clutch lets the rider disconnect the engine from the gearbox to start from rest, change gear and stop without stalling, and it limits the torque that can reach the drivetrain during a shock. A motorcycle clutch must transmit full engine torque in a package small enough to fit inside the crankcase cover, so it uses many small friction faces in parallel rather than one large disc as in a car. Running the plates in engine oil (a **wet clutch**) carries heat away from the faces and makes engagement smooth, at the cost of some drag and a lower friction coefficient than a dry clutch.

Pulling the lever pushes the lift rod and release bearing outward, compressing the springs and letting the plates separate.](photo:render-engine-xray.jpg "Render: Transitions Lab, CC BY-SA 4.0")

![Wet multiplate clutch exploded: clutch basket with primary driven gear and cushion springs, alternating friction and steel plates, clutch hub, pressure plate, coil springs and bolts, release bearing and pushrod mechanism.](fig:F13)

The parts, in the order the torque passes through them, are:

- **Clutch basket (outer, housing)**: a cup, usually aluminium, with axial slots around its rim. It is driven by the primary gear and turns freely on a bush or needle bearing on the gearbox mainshaft.
- **Friction plates (drive plates)**: aluminium or steel cores bonded with a cork, paper-based or sintered friction material, with tabs on the outside that engage the basket slots. A 125 cc engine typically has four or five; a 1,000 cc sports engine eight to ten.
- **Steel plates (driven plates)**: plain pressed steel discs with internal teeth that engage splines on the clutch hub, interleaved between the friction plates.
- **Clutch hub (centre, inner)**: splined to the gearbox mainshaft and held by a locknut with a tab washer or staked collar. The K-Light 125 manual calls this the "center bearing bracket".
- **Pressure plate**: clamps the plate stack against a flange on the hub. Four to six coil springs, held by bolts through the pressure plate into hub bosses, or a single diaphragm (Belleville) spring, provide the clamping force.
- **Release mechanism**: lifts the pressure plate away from the stack against the springs. On most singles the cable turns a lever on the right-hand crankcase cover whose cam or quick-thread pushes on a release bearing in the centre of the pressure plate (an outside push mechanism); on many larger engines a pushrod runs through the hollow mainshaft from a lever or slave cylinder on the left-hand side.

With the lever released, the springs squeeze the stack so that the friction plates (turning with the basket) and the steel plates (turning with the hub) lock together by friction, and torque flows from basket to hub to mainshaft. Pulling the lever compresses the springs and lets the stack open by a total of about 0.5–1.0 mm; the plates then slide past each other in a film of oil and the engine runs while the mainshaft can stop.

### Cable versus hydraulic release

A **cable release** is light, cheap and easy to replace at the roadside, but cable friction rises as the inner wire corrodes or the outer is routed in tight bends, and the free play changes as the plates wear and as the engine (and so the cable) warms. Free play is set at the lever (10–15 mm on the K-Light 125) with the in-line adjuster, using the lower adjuster at the crankcase when the upper one runs out of thread. Too little free play holds the release bearing against the pressure plate and causes slip; too much prevents full release and causes drag.

A **hydraulic release** uses a master cylinder on the handlebar and a slave cylinder at the engine, linked by a hose. Because the slave piston sits on the pushrod with the fluid taking up the clearance, plate wear is compensated automatically and there is no free play to set. The fluid is normally DOT 4 brake fluid (some makers, for example many Magura-equipped European bikes, specify mineral oil, which must never be mixed with glycol fluids), and the system is bled exactly like a brake (Job sheet 4.7.2).

### Slipper and assist clutches

A downshift at high road speed asks the rear wheel to drive the engine through the gearbox. If the engine cannot accelerate quickly enough, the back-torque can lock or hop the rear tyre. A **slipper clutch (back-torque limiter)** places a set of ramps between the hub and the pressure plate. Under drive torque the ramps are inactive or, in an **assist** design, they pull the pressure plate further into the stack, so lighter springs can be fitted for the same capacity and the lever effort falls by roughly a quarter to a third. Under back-torque the ramps ride up and push the pressure plate outwards, partially releasing the stack so that it slips at a set torque. Combined assist and slipper ("A&S") clutches are now common on mid-size road bikes and are fitted to some 125 cc and 150 cc sports models.

### Centrifugal clutches

Step-through motorcycles such as the Honda Super Cub use an **automatic centrifugal clutch** on the crankshaft: weighted shoes or a ball-and-ramp plate engage the plate stack as engine speed rises above idle, and a linkage from the gear pedal also releases the clutch while the pedal is moved, so the rider never needs a lever. Twist-and-go scooters place a dry **centrifugal shoe clutch** on the driven pulley of the CVT (Chapter 4.3): three shoes, held in by springs, swing outwards and contact the inside of a steel bell as engine speed rises, typically engaging between about 2,800 and 4,000 rpm depending on the spring rate. Mopeds and small trail bikes for children use similar dry shoe clutches on the crankshaft.

## Torque capacity

The torque a plate clutch can transmit before it slips is the friction force on each face multiplied by its effective radius and by the number of faces in contact.

![The same clutch exploded along the mainshaft, in assembly order from the crankcase outward: driven gear and basket with its six slots, friction and steel plates, splined hub, pressure plate, the four springs and their bolts, the release bearing and lift rod, and finally the clutch cover. The friction plates lock to the basket by their outer tabs and turn with the engine; the steel plates lock to the hub by their inner teeth and turn with the gearbox.](photo:render-engine-exploded.jpg "Render: Transitions Lab, CC BY-SA 4.0")

```eq
T = μ × F × r_m × n
r_m ≈ (r_o + r_i) / 2
```

where T is the slip torque (N·m), μ the coefficient of friction (about 0.10–0.15 for wet paper or cork against steel in motorcycle oil, about 0.3–0.4 dry), F the total spring force on the pressure plate (N), r_m the mean friction radius (m) using the uniform-wear approximation, r_o and r_i the outer and inner radii of the friction lining, and n the number of friction faces. Each friction plate has two faces, so five friction plates give n = 10.

Worked example: a 125 cc clutch has five friction plates with linings from r_i = 35 mm to r_o = 50 mm (r_m = 42.5 mm), four springs each exerting 240 N at installed height (F = 960 N) and μ = 0.13.

```eq
T = 0.13 × 960 × 0.0425 × 10 ≈ 53 N·m
```

The clutch must carry engine torque multiplied by the primary ratio: 8.9 × 3.40 ≈ 30 N·m for the K-Light 125. The capacity margin of 53/30 ≈ 1.75 covers firing-stroke torque peaks, which on a single can be several times the mean, and the loss of spring force as springs fatigue. The same equation explains every slip fault: worn linings reduce spring installed length and so F; short springs reduce F; glazed linings or the wrong oil reduce μ.

> [!WARNING] Oil and the wet clutch
> Automotive oils labelled "Energy Conserving" or with friction modifiers (molybdenum, some esters) lower μ and cause clutch slip. Use oil meeting **JASO MA or MA2**; JASO MB is for scooters with dry clutches and must **not** be used in a wet clutch. A clutch that slips after an oil change is checked by changing back to a JASO MA oil before dismantling anything.

## Slip, drag, judder and wear

**Slip** shows as engine speed rising without a matching rise in road speed, most noticeably in top gear at wide throttle. Causes in order of likelihood are insufficient free play, wrong oil, worn friction plates, weak springs and, on a cable clutch, a binding release lever. **Drag** is incomplete release: the bike creeps in gear with the lever pulled, neutral is hard to find at rest, and first gear engages with a loud clunk. Causes are excessive free play, a stretched cable, warped steel plates, swollen friction material, notched basket slots that stop the plates sliding apart, or cold, thick oil. **Judder** (grab) during take-off comes from glazed or contaminated linings, warped plates, a notched basket or loose engine mounts.

Steel plates rarely wear in thickness but distort from heat. Each is checked for warp on a surface plate with a feeler gauge; a typical service limit is **0.10–0.20 mm**, so any plate that admits a feeler of that size at any point is replaced. Friction plates are measured across the lining with a vernier at several points (K-Light 125 limit **2.6 mm** from 2.9–3.0 mm new); MAVCC Unit XIII notes that the wear on a single plate is small, but multiplied by eight or ten faces it is enough to shorten the spring installed height noticeably. Springs are measured for free length and replaced as a set if any one is below the limit, because uneven force tilts the pressure plate and causes judder. Basket slots and hub splines are inspected for notches worn by the plate tabs: light notches can be dressed with a file, deep notches mean a new basket.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Check | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Engine revs rise, speed does not (slip) | No free play; worn plates; weak springs; wrong oil | Free play at lever; plate thickness; spring free length; oil grade | Adjust to 10–15 mm; replace plates or springs as a set; JASO MA oil |
| Bike creeps with lever pulled (drag) | Excess free play; warped steels; notched basket | Free play; warp on surface plate; slot condition | Adjust; replace plates; dress or replace basket |
| Judder on take-off | Glazed or oily linings; warped plates; loose engine mount | Lining surface; warp; mount bolts | Replace plates; torque mounts |
| Heavy lever | Dry or kinked cable; release mechanism seized | Disconnect cable and operate lever alone | Lubricate or replace cable; re-route without tight bends |
| Clonk on throttle opening | Worn cushion springs; loose hub nut | Rotational play between primary gear and basket; hub nut torque | Replace basket assembly; re-torque and lock nut |

## The kickstart mechanism

Many small motorcycles keep a kickstarter as a backup to the electric starter; the K-Light 125 has both. The kick lever is splined to a **kickstart shaft** carrying a **ratchet** on a helical spline and a free-turning **kick gear** that meshes with a gear on the gearbox (usually the mainshaft first gear or an idler that drives the clutch basket). At rest, a tab on the ratchet rests against a **guide (stopper) plate** fixed to the crankcase, which holds the ratchet away from the kick gear. When the lever is pressed, the shaft turns, the tab leaves the stopper and a light **ratchet spring** pushes the ratchet along the helix until its face teeth engage the kick gear, so the shaft drives the gearbox and, through the engaged clutch, the crankshaft. When the engine fires and overruns the gear, the angled ratchet teeth ride over each other; at the end of the stroke a strong **return spring** brings the shaft back, the tab rides up the stopper and the ratchet is pulled clear. A **primary kickstart**, which drives the clutch basket directly, can start the engine in any gear with the clutch pulled; a non-primary type needs neutral. Faults are a lever that slips under load (worn ratchet teeth or a weak ratchet spring), a lever that does not return (broken or unhooked return spring) and grinding during riding (stopper worn so the ratchet does not fully disengage). The K-Light 125 manual directs checks of both springs and of the fit of the shaft spline in the ratchet.

1. Primary drive, gearbox and final drive. Placing a reduction ahead of the clutch lowers clutch and mainshaft speed, reducing centrifugal stress, oil churning losses and the inertia to be synchronised in a gear change.
2. i_p = 72/17 = 4.24. n_clutch = 8,000/4.24 ≈ 1,889 rpm.
3. (a) Friction plates (by their outer tabs). (b) Steel plates (by their inner teeth); the hub itself is splined to the mainshaft.
4. r_m = (0.038 + 0.055)/2 = 0.0465 m; F = 5 × 280 = 1,400 N; n = 12. T = 0.12 × 1,400 × 0.0465 × 12 ≈ 93.7 N·m.
5. F = 5 × 230 = 1,150 N; T = 0.12 × 1,150 × 0.0465 × 12 ≈ 77.0 N·m. The fall is (93.7 − 77.0)/93.7 ≈ 18 %, the same proportion as the fall in spring force.
6. Ramps between hub and pressure plate pull the pressure plate into the stack under drive torque, adding clamping force so weaker springs can be used; under back-torque the ramps push the pressure plate outwards, reducing clamping force so the clutch slips and limits the torque that can lock or hop the rear tyre.
7. Friction modifiers in energy-conserving car oils reduce the coefficient of friction between the plates, lowering torque capacity and causing slip; JASO MA and MA2 oils are tested for wet-clutch friction performance.

10. The return spring rotates the shaft back; a tab on the ratchet rides onto the fixed guide (stopper) plate, which pulls the ratchet along its helical spline and away from the kick gear.

## Why a gearbox is needed

An engine produces useful torque over a limited speed band; the K-Light 125 makes 8.9 N·m at 7,500 rpm and 7.8 kW at 9,000 rpm and runs out of breath not far beyond. The gearbox selects a ratio that keeps the engine inside that band from walking pace to top speed and multiplies torque at low road speed so the machine can start on a hill with a passenger. Five or six ratios are normal on road motorcycles; competition motocross bikes use five closely spaced ratios, trials bikes six with a very low first gear, and scooters replace the gearbox with a continuously variable transmission (Chapter 4.3).

## Constant-mesh layout

Almost every motorcycle gearbox is a **constant-mesh, two-shaft, sequential** type built into the crankcase (unit construction) and lubricated by engine oil. The **mainshaft (input shaft, primary shaft)** carries the clutch and receives power from it. The **countershaft (output shaft, layshaft, auxiliary shaft)** lies parallel and behind it and carries the gearbox (front) sprocket on its outer end. Each gear pair is always in mesh; what changes is which pair is locked to its shaft.

On each shaft some gears are **fixed** (machined integral with the shaft or pressed on), some are **free** (turning on a bush or needle bearing and located axially by circlips and thrust washers), and some are **sliding** gears that are splined to the shaft so they always turn with it but can move axially. A sliding gear has **dogs** (projecting lugs) on one or both faces that engage **dog slots** or matching dogs on the face of the neighbouring free gear. When a fork slides the gear across, the dogs lock the free gear to the shaft and that ratio carries the torque; all the other pairs merely idle. The sliding gears double as gears in their own right, which keeps the box short, so on a five-speed box there are typically three sliding members serving five ratios.

Because the gears are always meshed, they can use strong, quiet helical or spur teeth, and shifting only requires the dogs to line up, not the teeth. The price is that a dog engagement is abrupt and that the gearbox has no synchromesh, so the rider must unload the dogs momentarily with the clutch or a throttle lift.

### Dogs and dog wear

Dogs transmit the full torque of the gear across a few square millimetres of contact, so the shapes matter. Road gearboxes commonly use three to six dogs per face with their driving faces **back-cut (undercut)** by 2–4°, so that the drive torque tends to pull the gears together rather than push them apart. As the driving edges wear and round off, the back-cut is lost and the axial component reverses: under load the gears are pushed out of engagement. Rounded dogs are therefore the root cause of almost every case of jumping out of gear. The K-Light 125 manual lists "joint claws are worn and the edges become round corners" as the first cause of the gear disengaging spontaneously. Race gearboxes use fewer, wider dogs with large clearances (for example three dogs into six slots) so that engagement is faster at the cost of more backlash.

## The selector mechanism

### Shift drum and forks

![Gear shift mechanism: shift drum with tracks, forks, shift shaft, ratchet, detent star, stopper arm and neutral switch.](fig:TR01)

The **shift drum** is a cylinder with machined grooves (cam tracks) around its circumference. Each **shift fork** has a guide pin that rides in one groove and two prongs that sit in a groove on a sliding gear. As the drum rotates, each groove moves its fork, and so its sliding gear, sideways by a precise amount at the right moment. Drum position therefore defines the gear. On most road boxes two forks run on the mainshaft side or the countershaft side on a separate **fork shaft**, and a third on the other.

> [!SPEC] K-Light 125 gearshift mechanism limits (K-Light 125 manual)
> Fork shaft outside diameter 11.97–12.00 mm, limit **11.95 mm**. Fork bore 12.000–12.018 mm, limit **12.05 mm**. Fork claw (prong) thickness 4.93–5.00 mm, limit **4.7 mm**. Shift drum outside diameter 35.8–36.0 mm, limit **35.75 mm**. Drum groove width 7.05–7.15 mm, limit **7.3 mm**.

Fork prongs rub continually against the side of the gear groove when a gear is loaded slightly askew, and they wear thin or turn blue from heat. A worn prong, a worn drum groove or a bent fork reduces the distance the dog is pushed into engagement, which leads straight to jumping out of gear.

### Ratchet, stopper and detent

The gear pedal is splined to a **gearshift shaft** that runs through the crankcase. On the inside, a **shift arm (shift plate)** with a pair of spring-loaded **pawls (claws)** engages pins or ramps on the end of the drum. Each stroke of the pedal rotates the drum by one step; a **return (centring) spring** then brings shaft and arm back to the centre, ready for the next stroke. This is what makes the box **sequential**: each pedal stroke can only move one gear up or down.

Drum position is held between shifts by a **stopper arm (detent lever)** with a roller, pressed by a spring into notches in a star-shaped **detent (positioning) plate** on the drum. The notch profile pulls the drum fully into position at the end of each shift, so a half-completed pedal movement either completes or falls back. The neutral position is usually a half-step between first and second, which is why the pedal moves only half as far from first to neutral, and why a hurried first-to-second shift can stop in neutral. A **neutral switch** in the crankcase is earthed by a contact on the drum in neutral, lighting the green lamp and, on bikes with starter interlocks, permitting the starter to turn with the clutch engaged (Module 3).

The K-Light 125 manual lists the parts of this mechanism as the gearshift shaft assembly, shift plate and spring, return spring, stopper lever and spring, and positioning plate, and directs that the shift plate must return freely before dismantling, that the return spring be checked for weakness, and that the shaft be checked for bending.

### Neutral finders

Finding neutral at a standstill is awkward because the drum can skip past the half-step. Kawasaki's **Positive Neutral Finder** places steel balls in radial holes in a countershaft gear; when the shaft is stationary they drop into a groove and block the gear from sliding beyond neutral towards second, but when the shaft turns, centrifugal force throws them out and the shift is unaffected. Other makers achieve a similar result with detent geometry that makes the neutral notch deeper.

### Quickshifters

A **quickshifter** allows full-throttle upshifts without the clutch. A load cell or strain gauge in the gear linkage (or a switch with a set pre-load) detects the rider's pedal pressure and signals the ECU, which interrupts ignition or injection for a period that shortens with engine speed, typically from about 80–100 ms at low rpm to about 50 ms near the limiter. The momentary loss of drive torque unloads the dogs, the spring-loaded drum completes the shift, and power is restored. A **bidirectional** (up-and-down) quickshifter also blips the throttle via ride-by-wire on downshifts to match revs. Quickshifters need positive dog engagement and correct linkage free play; a sloppy linkage or worn detent produces missed shifts and false neutrals.

## Ratio calculations

Each gear ratio is the driven gear's tooth count divided by the driving gear's. Because power passes through three stages in series, the ratios multiply.

```eq
i_gear = z_countershaft gear / z_mainshaft gear
i_final = z_rear sprocket / z_front sprocket
i_overall = i_p × i_gear × i_final
n_wheel = n_engine / i_overall
v (km/h) = n_wheel × C × 60 / 1000
```

where C is the rolling circumference of the rear tyre in metres. The geometric diameter of a tyre is the rim diameter plus twice the sidewall height; the sidewall height is the section width multiplied by the aspect ratio. A tyre under load rolls on a slightly smaller effective radius, and a reduction of about 3 % from the geometric circumference is a reasonable working allowance.

### Worked example: road speed in each gear

The K-Light 125 manual gives the rear sprocket as 41 teeth, the chain as 428 pitch with 134 links and the rear tyre as 130/90-15, but does not list the primary or internal gear ratios or the front sprocket. The calculation below therefore uses a representative set typical of air-cooled 125 cc singles of this class, together with an assumed 14-tooth gearbox sprocket; the method is the same with the real figures from the parts book or from counting teeth.

Rear tyre 130/90-15: sidewall = 130 × 0.90 = 117 mm; diameter = 15 × 25.4 + 2 × 117 = 615 mm; geometric circumference = π × 0.615 = 1.932 m; rolling circumference C ≈ 1.932 × 0.97 ≈ 1.874 m.

Primary ratio 68/20 = 3.400; final drive 41/14 = 2.929.

| Gear | Teeth (driven/driving) | Gear ratio | Overall ratio | km/h at 7,500 rpm | km/h at 9,000 rpm |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 33/12 | 2.750 | 27.38 | 30.8 | 37.0 |
| 2 | 25/14 | 1.786 | 17.78 | 47.4 | 56.9 |
| 3 | 26/19 | 1.368 | 13.63 | 61.9 | 74.3 |
| 4 | 24/22 | 1.091 | 10.86 | 77.6 | 93.2 |
| 5 | 18/20 | 0.900 | 8.96 | 94.1 | 112.9 |

Taking fifth gear as an example: i_overall = 3.400 × 0.900 × 2.929 = 8.96; at 9,000 rpm the wheel turns at 9,000/8.96 = 1,004 rpm, and v = 1,004 × 1.874 × 60/1,000 = 112.9 km/h.

The manual quotes a maximum speed of 90 km/h. With these ratios the engine would reach its power peak in fifth only at 113 km/h, which aerodynamic and rolling drag at 7.8 kW will not allow, so the machine tops out at about 7,200 rpm in fifth, below the power peak. This is deliberate on commuter machines: an "overdrive" top gear lowers cruising rpm, noise and fuel consumption at the price of top speed. Fitting one tooth fewer on the front sprocket (13T) raises i_final to 3.154 and lowers the 9,000 rpm speed in fifth to 105 km/h, bringing the power peak closer to the speed the bike can actually reach and improving acceleration in every gear.

The step between gears is the ratio of adjacent gear ratios: 2.750/1.786 = 1.54 from first to second, then 1.31, 1.25 and 1.21. Steps shrink towards top gear because, at higher speed, the power needed rises steeply and the engine must drop fewer revs on each upshift to stay near its power band; this approximately geometric progression is normal design practice.

## Gearbox faults

| Symptom | Likely cause | Check | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jumps out of a particular gear under load or on the overrun | Rounded or worn dogs and slots on that gear pair; worn or bent fork; worn drum groove; weak stopper spring | Inspect dogs for rounding; measure fork claw thickness (K-Light limit 4.7 mm) and drum groove (limit 7.3 mm); stopper spring | Replace both gears of the dog pair, the fork and if needed the drum; replace spring |

| Pedal does not return | Broken or unhooked return spring; bent shaft rubbing the case | Remove clutch cover and observe the spring | Replace spring or shaft |

A gear that jumps out has usually been damaging its dogs for some time, and a rider who holds the lever with a boot to stop it accelerates the wear. Dogs are **always** replaced in pairs (sliding gear and its partner free gear), because a new gear against a rounded partner wears to the same shape quickly.

> Many shifting complaints are clutch complaints. Before splitting the crankcase, confirm that the clutch fully releases (free play, plate warp), that the oil is the correct grade and level, and that the external linkage moves freely; the K-Light 125 manual lists "clutch is not completely disengaged" as the first cause of difficult shifting.

## Roller chain

A roller chain is built from alternating **inner links** (two inner plates pressed onto two **bushings**, with a free-turning **roller** on each bushing) and **outer links** (two outer plates pressed onto two **pins** that pass through the bushings). The pin-to-bushing joint is the wear surface: as it wears, every pitch lengthens slightly and the chain "stretches". In a **plain chain** the joint is lubricated only by what the rider sprays on. An **O-ring chain** traps factory grease in each joint with rubber rings between the inner and outer plates; an **X-ring** (or similar quad-lip) chain uses a ring of X-shaped section that touches the plates on four smaller lips, reducing friction and drag while sealing at least as well. Sealed chains typically last two to three times as long as plain chains on road machines, at the cost of a little extra friction and width.

![Final drive types compared: chain and sprockets, toothed belt and pulleys, and shaft drive with bevel gears in section.](fig:TR02)

![A 428 O-ring chain on a 42-tooth rear sprocket: the chain wraps half the sprocket, each roller sits in a tooth seat on the pitch circle, gold outer plates alternate with dark inner plates, and the sprocket is bolted to the hub carrier with six nuts.](photo:render-chain.jpg "Render: Transitions Lab, CC BY-SA 4.0")

![Chain final drive: gearbox and rear sprockets with the chain run, a section through an O-ring chain showing pin, bushing, roller, inner and outer plates and O-rings, slack measured at the midpoint of the lower run, and a 20-link length measurement for wear.](fig:F15)

The three-digit size code gives pitch and roller width. The first digit is the pitch in eighths of an inch and the last two digits the roller width in eightieths of an inch.

| Size | Pitch | Roller width | Typical use | Typical tensile strength |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 415 | 12.70 mm (1/2 in) | 4.76 mm (3/16 in) | Mopeds, 50 cc racers | 10–15 kN |
| 428 | 12.70 mm | 7.94 mm (5/16 in) | 100–200 cc commuters, K-Light 125 | 17–22 kN |
| 520 | 15.875 mm (5/8 in) | 6.35 mm (1/4 in) | 250–700 cc, most off-road | 30–40 kN |
| 525 | 15.875 mm | 7.94 mm | 600–1,000 cc road | 35–45 kN |
| 530 | 15.875 mm | 9.53 mm (3/8 in) | Large road bikes | 40–50 kN |

Tensile values vary by maker and grade; the chain's catalogue figure is the one to use. The K-Light 125 chain is a 428 type with 134 links (428HO-1×134), so its nominal length is 134 × 12.70 = 1,701.8 mm. Chains are joined either by a **spring clip** master link, whose open end must face away from the direction of travel (MAVCC Unit XI), or by a **rivet link** peened with a press tool; sealed chains on larger bikes are riveted because a clip can come off at high speed, and MAVCC notes that the master link is usually the point of failure.

### Sprockets and ratio

Final-drive ratio is the rear sprocket teeth divided by the front sprocket teeth. Small front sprockets wear fastest because each tooth engages more often and the chain articulates through a larger angle, which also raises friction and polygon effect (speed pulsation as the chain rises and falls on the flats). For a given ratio, larger sprockets give longer life and smoother running but cost ground clearance and weight; a front sprocket below 13–14 teeth on a 428 or 520 chain is avoided on road bikes for this reason. A worn sprocket shows hooked or pointed teeth leaning against the direction of rotation; the K-Light 125 manual calls for replacement when about a quarter of the tooth form is worn. Because a stretched chain rides high on the teeth and wears them to its own pitch, chain and both sprockets are **replaced together**.

### Belt drive

Toothed (synchronous) belts with carbon or aramid tensile cords have been used on Harley-Davidson models since 1980 and are now common on electric motorcycles because they are quiet, clean and need no lubrication. A belt is wider than a chain for the same load and cannot be split, so the swingarm must be removable or open at one side. Belts are inspected for missing or cracked teeth, frayed edges and stone damage on the pulley faces; MAVCC notes that a stretched belt usually results from overtightening or misalignment. Tension is set with a gauge at a specified force and deflection, not by feel.

### Shaft drive

A shaft drive uses a bevel gear set at the gearbox (unless the crankshaft lies along the frame, as on BMW boxers and Moto Guzzi V-twins), a drive shaft with a universal joint at the swingarm pivot and a sliding spline to accommodate length change, and a bevel (crown-wheel-and-pinion) set in a housing at the wheel, lubricated by its own gear oil, typically an 80W-90 hypoid oil changed at specified intervals. Shafts are sealed and need little service but are heavier, lose about 2–4 % more power than a clean chain, and cannot easily change ratio.

The reaction torque at the rear bevel housing tries to rotate the housing around the wheel axle. On a simple swingarm that reaction lifts the rear of the machine on acceleration and lets it squat on closing the throttle: the **shaft-drive jacking effect**. Designs such as BMW's Paralever (introduced on the R80GS and R100GS in 1987) and Moto Guzzi's CARC add a torque arm that forms a parallelogram with the swingarm, so the bevel housing cannot rotate relative to the frame and the reaction is fed into the chassis.

## Scooter CVT

A twist-and-go scooter uses a **continuously variable transmission** consisting of two variable-diameter V-belt pulleys inside the left-hand side of the swinging engine unit, with a dry centrifugal clutch and a fixed reduction gearbox (typically about 9:1 to 11:1 in two stages, in an oil bath) to the rear wheel.

![Scooter CVT: drive pulley with variator rollers and ramp plate, V-belt, driven pulley with torque cam, contra spring and centrifugal clutch, shown in low ratio and high ratio positions.](fig:F16)

- **Drive pulley (variator)**: on the crankshaft. The fixed outer half faces a movable half whose back carries ramps. Six **rollers** (weights) sit between those ramps and a fixed **ramp plate**. As engine speed rises, centrifugal force drives the rollers outwards, they wedge between ramp plate and ramps, and push the movable half towards the fixed half, so the belt climbs to a larger radius.
- **Belt**: a cogged, aramid-corded rubber V-belt of fixed length. As it climbs the drive pulley it must sink deeper into the driven pulley.
- **Driven pulley**: the movable half is pushed closed by a strong **contra (torque) spring** and is guided on its hub by a helical **torque cam** (slots with guide pins). Belt tension tries to open it; drive torque through the cam tries to close it. When the rider opens the throttle hard at speed, the higher torque makes the cam squeeze the belt outwards on the driven pulley, which forces a lower ratio: an automatic kickdown.
- **Centrifugal clutch**: on the driven pulley, its shoes engage the clutch bell attached to the gearbox input shaft.

At rest the drive pulley is open (small belt radius) and the driven pulley closed (large radius), so the belt ratio is low, typically about 2.4–2.8:1. At full speed the positions reverse to about 0.8–0.9:1, a total spread of about 3:1. Because the variator decides the ratio from engine speed, the engine runs at a nearly constant rpm while the scooter accelerates, set by the balance between roller centrifugal force and belt and contra-spring forces.

### Tuning roller weights

Lighter rollers need more rpm to generate the force to close the variator, so the engine revs higher during acceleration (closer to peak power) and the scooter accelerates harder but cruises noisier and uses more fuel. Heavier rollers shift up at lower rpm for a calmer, more economical ride but less acceleration, especially uphill. Typical 125 cc scooters use six rollers of about 13–20 g in sizes such as 16 × 13 mm or 20 × 15 mm; a change of 1–2 g per roller moves the acceleration rpm by very roughly a few hundred rpm. A stiffer contra spring raises the rpm at which the belt can move on the driven pulley and so has a similar effect at the upper end. Rollers wear flat spots that make them slide rather than roll, which shows as a ratio that changes in steps and a loss of top speed.

## Job sheet 4.3.1: Chain and sprocket inspection, adjustment and lubrication

| Tools and materials | Reference values |
|---|---|
| Centre or paddock stand, steel rule, vernier caliper or 300 mm rule, axle and adjuster spanners, torque wrench, chain cleaner (neutral, non-solvent), chain lubricant, soft brush, gloves, safety glasses | K-Light 125: slack 20–40 mm; chain 428, 134 links; rear sprocket 41T; rear axle nut 100–113 N·m; sprocket nuts 45–50 N·m (K-Light 125 manual) |

1. Switch off the ignition, select neutral and support the machine upright on level ground (the K-Light 125 manual specifies upright, not on the side stand). **Never** turn the wheel by pulling the chain with the engine running; fingers are drawn into the sprocket.
2. Rotate the rear wheel slowly and find the tightest point of the chain (chains and sprockets wear unevenly). At that point, push the lower run up and down at its midpoint and measure the total vertical movement with the rule. K-Light 125: **20–40 mm**. Less than 20 mm overloads the gearbox output bearing and the chain once the suspension compresses; more than 40 mm lets the chain whip and jump teeth.
3. Look for **tight spots**: if slack varies by more than about **10 mm** around the run, the chain or sprocket is unevenly worn and adjustment will not correct it.
4. Check for kinked (stiff) links by running the chain over a finger on the lower run, and inspect rollers, side plates and O-rings for cracks, missing rollers, rust and dry joints. The K-Light 125 manual directs replacement of a chain with damaged rollers or plates or hard (seized) joints that cleaning and lubrication do not free.
5. Measure wear: pull the chain taut and measure across 10 pitches from the far side of roller 1 to the near side of roller 11 (the K-Light 125 method), or across 20 pitches. For 12.70 mm pitch the new 10-pitch length is 127.0 mm and the 20-pitch length 254.0 mm; the K-Light 125 manual quotes 127 mm, which is the **new** length, so apply the chain maker's elongation limit, typically about **1.5–2 %** (about **129 mm** over 10 pitches, **258–259 mm** over 20). Measure in two or three places.
6. Inspect the sprockets for hooked, pointed or broken teeth and for loose rear sprocket nuts. Check the cush drive rubbers in the hub for cracks and free play (more than a few millimetres of rotational movement of the sprocket carrier against the wheel).
7. To adjust, loosen the rear axle nut and the adjuster locknuts, then turn both adjusters by equal amounts until slack is within the specified range at the tight spot. Check that the alignment marks on both swingarm adjusters show the same position, and sight along the chain from behind the rear sprocket to confirm it runs straight.
8. Tighten the axle nut to **100–113 N·m** (K-Light 125), then the adjuster locknuts. Recheck slack, which often changes slightly as the axle nut tightens.
9. Check the rear brake: on drum-braked machines moving the axle changes the brake rod geometry and pedal free play (Job sheet 4.7.3); on discs check that the caliper bracket is located and the wheel spins freely.
10. Clean the chain with a neutral cleaner and a soft brush. The K-Light 125 manual warns against steam, petrol or solvents, which swell or dissolve O-rings. Dry it, then apply chain lubricant to the inside of the lower run where the plates overlap, so centrifugal force carries it into the joints, and wipe off excess. Lubricate warm, after a ride, and every 500–800 km or after riding in rain.

**Pass criteria:** slack 20–40 mm at the tightest point with no more than about 10 mm variation; 10-pitch length within the chain maker's limit; no hooked teeth or damaged rollers; adjusters equal and wheel aligned; axle nut at specified torque; chain clean and lubricated; brake free play correct.

## Job sheet 4.3.2: CVT belt and roller inspection

| Tools and materials | Reference values |
|---|---|
| Scooter service manual, variator holding tool, impact or socket set, clutch spring compressor (for the driven pulley), vernier caliper, clean rags, brake cleaner, high-temperature grease for the variator boss only | Belt width and roller outside diameter limits from the manual; typical belt width service limit about 1 mm below new; rollers replaced as a set when flat spots appear |

![A scooter unit-swing engine on the centre stand with the CVT cover removed: the finned fan of the drive pulley, the cogged V-belt and the driven pulley with its clutch bell sit directly in front of the rear wheel, and the swingarm pivot is visible at the front of the crankcase.](photo:scooter-cvt-workshop.jpg "Photograph: Transitions Lab collection")

1. With the engine cold, remove the CVT cover bolts and cover, noting the position of any air intake duct. Clean belt dust from the case with a vacuum, not compressed air; the dust from older belts and clutch linings is a respiratory hazard.
2. Hold the variator with the holding tool and remove the drive pulley nut and fixed half. Slide the belt off, then withdraw the movable half with its boss and rollers, keeping the ramp plate assembly together so the rollers do not fall out.
3. Measure the belt width across the top face at several points. Compare with the service limit; a belt below the limit runs lower in the drive pulley and higher in the driven pulley, so the scooter loses top speed. Replace the belt if it is cracked across the cogs, glazed, frayed or shows oil contamination.
4. Measure each roller's outside diameter and inspect for flat spots or worn cover. Replace all rollers as a set. Check the ramp faces and ramp plate guides for grooving.
5. Check the boss and the movable half bore for wear (the half should slide without rocking). Lightly grease the boss bore with the specified grease, keeping grease off belt faces.
6. Inspect the driven pulley: guide pins and torque cam slots for wear, contra spring free length, clutch shoe lining thickness and the bell for scoring or out-of-round. Replace shoes with less than about 1.5–2 mm of lining or the manual limit.
7. Clean both pulley faces with brake cleaner. Reassemble, fitting the belt with any direction arrow facing forward, pressing the driven pulley open by hand so the belt sits deep in it before tightening the variator nut to specification.
8. Rotate the engine with the kickstarter or starter several times to seat the belt, refit the cover, and road test: note engine rpm during full-throttle acceleration and top speed.

**Pass criteria:** belt width above the limit with no cracks or glazing; rollers round and of equal size; variator slides freely; clutch lining and bell within limits; correct acceleration rpm and no slip or judder on take-off.

# 4.4 Frame and steering geometry

## Frame types and materials

The frame locates the steering axis, the swingarm pivot and the engine relative to each other and must hold that relationship under braking, cornering and bump loads. The quantities that matter are **torsional stiffness** between steering head and swingarm pivot (resistance to twisting, typically several kN·m per degree on a road machine), **lateral stiffness** (which designers deliberately limit on sports bikes so the chassis can flex to absorb bumps when leaned over and the suspension is working at an angle), and mass.

![A classic double-cradle steel frame, the layout of most air-cooled roadsters.](photo:r3d-frame_cradle.png "Render: Transitions Lab, CC BY-SA 4.0")

- **Steel tube cradle**: one or two tubes run down from the steering head under the engine (single or double cradle) and back to the swingarm pivot. Mild or chromoly steel tube is cheap, easy to weld and repair, and tolerant of fatigue; most commuter machines such as the Honda CG125 and the K-Light 125 use a welded steel tube frame with the engine as a hung load.
- **Backbone (spine)**: a single large tube or pressed-steel spine from the head over the engine, with the engine hung below. Used on step-throughs (pressed-steel spine of the Super Cub) and many small trail bikes.
- **Aluminium twin-spar (beam, perimeter)**: two large extruded or cast aluminium beams run in straight lines from the head around the engine to the pivot, the shortest load path, which gives high torsional stiffness at low mass. Aluminium has a third of the density and a third of the stiffness of steel, so sections are larger and walls thicker; it has no fatigue endurance limit and is harder to repair. Used on sports bikes and modern motocross machines.
- **Trellis**: a lattice of short straight steel (sometimes aluminium) tubes forming triangulated panels, giving high stiffness with thin tube; associated with Ducati and KTM.
- **Stressed engine and monocoque designs**: the engine crankcase carries load directly, with small subframes at front and rear; cast aluminium or carbon "frameless" designs follow the same idea. Electric motorcycles often use the battery enclosure as a structural member.

A frame that has been crashed is checked on a jig or by measuring between the steering head and swingarm pivot and from the head to the rear axle on both sides; small differences of a few millimetres side to side indicate a twist that will make the machine pull or weave.

## Steering geometry

![Steering geometry: steering axis at rake angle to the vertical, fork offset between steering axis and wheel spindle, trail from the axis intersection to the contact patch, wheelbase between contact patches, with dimension lines.](fig:F17)

- **Rake (caster angle)**: the angle of the steering axis from vertical. Typical values are about 23–25° for sports bikes, 25–27° for commuters and standards, 26–28° for off-road and 30–34° for cruisers.
- **Fork offset**: the perpendicular distance from the steering axis to the front wheel spindle, created by the triple clamps and sometimes by an offset spindle position at the fork foot.
- **Trail**: the distance along the ground from the point where the steering axis meets the ground to the centre of the front tyre contact patch, which lies behind it. Typical values are 85–100 mm for sports bikes, 90–110 mm for commuters and 120–150 mm for cruisers.
- **Wheelbase**: distance between wheel spindles (1,440 mm on the K-Light 125).

```eq
trail = (R × sin θ − d) / cos θ
```

where R is the loaded front tyre radius, θ the rake angle and d the fork offset.

Worked example: a K-Light 125-type front wheel has a 90/90-17 tyre; diameter = 431.8 + 2 × 81 = 593.8 mm, so R ≈ 297 mm unloaded (a few millimetres less loaded). The manual does not state rake or offset, so assume θ = 26° and d = 30 mm as typical for a 125 cc road bike.

```eq
trail = (297 × 0.4384 − 30) / 0.8988 = (130.2 − 30) / 0.8988 ≈ 111 mm
```

Trail is what makes the front wheel self-centring: because the contact patch trails behind the steering axis, the tyre's side force acts on a lever arm that turns the wheel back into line, like a shopping-trolley caster. More trail and more rake give more straight-line stability and a heavier, slower steering response; less trail and a steeper head give quicker steering but less resistance to disturbances, increasing the risk of **wobble** (a 4–10 Hz front-end oscillation, mostly at medium speeds) or **kickback** (tank-slapper) when the front wheel lands askew after a bump under acceleration. A longer wheelbase adds stability and reduces weight transfer (less wheelie and stoppie tendency) but needs more steering input to turn. Geometry changes dynamically: under braking the fork compresses, the rake steepens and trail falls by 10–20 mm, which is why bikes steer more quickly on the brakes. Fitting a taller rear shock or sliding the fork tubes up in the clamps produces the same effect statically.

A **steering damper** is a small hydraulic damper (linear or rotary) between frame and lower clamp that resists rapid steering movements while having little effect at the slow rates of normal steering. It suppresses kickback on sports bikes and on off-road machines hitting rocks. Electronic dampers vary their damping with speed and acceleration. A damper treats the symptom: wobble on a road bike that did not previously wobble is first investigated by checking tyres, pressures, wheel alignment, steering head bearings, fork alignment in the clamps and luggage loading.

## Steering head bearings

The steering stem, welded or pressed into the lower clamp, turns in two bearings in the frame's head tube.

- **Loose ball bearings (cup and cone)** use steel balls running between pressed races in the head tube and on the stem. They are cheap and common on commuter machines. The K-Light 125 uses 1/4 in balls, with 21 in one race and 17 to 19 in the other according to the manual's tables. Because each ball makes point contact, the races are prone to **brinelling** (indentations where the balls sit while riding straight ahead), which makes the steering notchy at the centre.
- **Taper roller bearings** carry the load on line contact and handle the combined radial and axial loads of braking far better; MAVCC Unit XI notes that their pre-load is set by the torque applied to the adjusting nut. They are standard on larger machines and a common upgrade for ball-type heads.
- **Angular contact ball bearings** in cartridge form are used on some modern machines.

Bearings that are too loose let the front end clunk under braking and allow wobble; bearings that are too tight make the bike steer heavily and weave at low speed, because the front wheel cannot make its small self-correcting movements. Balls, races and dust seals are **always** replaced as a set (K-Light 125 manual), and a new lower race on the stem must be driven on square; the manual warns that a race fitted obliquely wears the stem.

> [!SPEC] K-Light 125 steering head (K-Light 125 manual)

# 4.5 Suspension

> [!OBJECTIVES]
> - Explain sprung and unsprung mass, spring rate and preload, and calculate spring force and sag.
> - Describe how compression and rebound damping are generated by orifice and shim-stack valving in damper-rod, cartridge and upside-down forks.
> - Compare twin-shock and linkage monoshock rear suspension and explain rising rate.
> - Measure and set static and rider sag, and change fork oil and seals to the K-Light 125 manual.

## Mass, springs and preload

**Sprung mass** is everything carried by the springs (frame, engine, rider, fuel); **unsprung mass** is everything that moves with the wheel (wheel, tyre, brake disc and caliper, fork lower legs, about half the swingarm, shock and chain). The lower the unsprung mass relative to the sprung mass, the less force a bump transmits to the chassis and the faster the tyre can follow the road surface, which is why cast aluminium wheels, light discs and USD forks (with the heavy outer tube on top) are used on sports machines.

![Conventional telescopic fork with triple clamps, stanchions and sliders.](photo:r3d-forks.png "Render: Transitions Lab, CC BY-SA 4.0")

The spring supports the sprung mass and stores bump energy. Its **spring rate** k is the force per unit compression, in N/mm.

```eq
F = k × x
x_preload = initial compression when fully extended
```

**Preload** is compression applied to the spring when the suspension is fully extended, by a threaded collar, a stepped cam ring (common on twin shocks) or a spacer under the fork cap. Preload does **not** change spring rate; it changes the load at which the suspension starts to move and so the ride height and sag. A heavier rider or a passenger needs more preload to restore sag; if the correct sag cannot be reached within the adjuster's range, the spring rate is wrong. Progressive (variable-pitch) fork springs have closely wound coils that become coil-bound early, so the rate rises with travel.

## Sag

**Sag** is how far the suspension compresses from fully extended under load. **Static sag** (free sag) is with the bike alone; **rider sag** (race sag) is with the rider in riding position. Typical targets:

| Machine | Front rider sag | Rear rider sag | Rear static sag |
|---|---|---|---|
| Road and commuter | 25–35 % of travel | 25–33 % of travel | 5–15 mm |
| Sports, track | 25–30 mm | 25–30 mm | 0–10 mm |
| Motocross, enduro (300 mm travel) | 60–70 mm | 95–105 mm | 30–40 mm |

Static sag checks spring rate: with correct rider sag, too little static sag (bike barely settles under its own weight) means the spring is too soft and has been preloaded heavily to compensate; too much static sag means it is too stiff.

## Damping

A spring alone would let the wheel bounce at the natural frequency of the system (about 1–2 Hz for the sprung mass, 10–15 Hz for the unsprung). The **damper** converts motion into heat by forcing oil through restrictions. **Compression damping** resists the wheel moving up; **rebound damping** resists the spring pushing it back down and is normally the stronger of the two, since the spring stores energy that must be released in a controlled way. Too little rebound damping gives a pogo or wallowing ride; too much makes the suspension "pack down" over successive bumps, losing travel and grip.

![An inverted (USD) fork: the large-diameter outer tubes are clamped in the yokes and the slim stanchions carry the axle.](photo:r3d-forks_usd.png "Render: Transitions Lab, CC BY-SA 4.0")

- **Orifice damping**: oil flows through fixed holes. Resistance rises with the square of shaft velocity, so it is soft at low speeds (poor body control) and harsh on sharp bumps. Damper-rod forks and cheap shocks use this principle.
- **Shim-stack valving**: oil flows through ports in a piston covered by a stack of thin spring-steel shims that bend open as pressure rises. The force curve can be shaped to be firm at low velocity and nearly linear at high velocity. Adjusters vary a bleed (bypass) needle that controls low-speed flow, and sometimes a preload on the shim stack for high-speed flow.

### Fork types

A **damper-rod fork** has a tube (the damper rod) bolted to the bottom of the slider, with holes that meter oil and a piston ring at the top sliding inside the fork tube. It is simple and cheap and is fitted to the K-Light 125 and most commuter machines. A **cartridge fork** uses a sealed internal tube with shim-stacked compression and rebound pistons, giving far better control and adjustability; open-cartridge and closed-cartridge (pressurised, to prevent cavitation) types exist, and many modern forks separate the functions into one leg for damping and the other for the spring. An **upside-down (USD, inverted) fork** places the large tube at the top in the clamps and the slim inner tube at the bottom, which improves bending stiffness where the bending moment is highest and reduces unsprung mass, but leaves the seals exposed to stones and dirt.

![Suspension: cutaway telescopic damper-rod fork with spring, damper rod, oil level, bushings, oil seal and dust seal, and a rear monoshock with rising-rate linkage beside a twin shock, with sag measurement points.](fig:F18)

Fork oil does the damping, so **viscosity and level** set the behaviour. Viscosity controls damping force; the "W" ratings of fork oils are not standardised between brands, so compare kinematic viscosity in mm²/s (cSt) at 40 °C. The K-Light 125 manual specifies 46# shock absorber oil (an ISO VG 46 viscosity grade, about 46 mm²/s at 40 °C) at **285 ± 2 ml per leg**. The air gap above the oil acts as a progressive air spring at the end of travel: a higher level (or larger volume) increases bottoming resistance. MAVCC Unit XI notes that milky fork oil means water has entered past failed seals.

> [!SPEC] K-Light 125 front fork (K-Light 125 manual)
> Damper-rod fork with oil seal 37 × 50 × 11 mm. Fork spring free length **300 mm, minimum 295 mm**. Oil 46# shock absorber oil, **285 ± 2 ml per leg**. Damper rod bolt 15–20 N·m with thread-locking compound. Fork cap bolt 12–18 N·m. Lower clamp bolts 45–50 N·m (assembly table) and upper clamp bolts 22–29 N·m. Rear shock upper nut 45–50 N·m, lower bolt 22–29 N·m; swingarm pivot nut 85–98 N·m.

## Rear suspension

**Twin shocks** mount from the swingarm near the axle to the frame rails; they are simple, cheap and give a nearly constant (linear) ratio between wheel and shock movement. The K-Light 125 uses twin shocks with preload adjustment. A **monoshock** uses one shock on the centreline, either mounted directly to the swingarm (as on many small road bikes and the Yamaha Monocross pattern) or through a **linkage** of rocker and pull rod. The linkage gives a **rising rate**: the shock moves little for each millimetre of wheel travel near full extension and more near full compression, so the suspension is soft on small bumps and resists bottoming on large ones. The **motion ratio** (wheel travel/shock travel) is typically 2–3:1, so the shock spring rate is several times the effective wheel rate; for a linear ratio R the wheel rate is k_shock/R².

![A tapered aluminium box-section swingarm with chain adjusters, pivot and linkage mounts.](photo:r3d-swingarm.png "Render: Transitions Lab, CC BY-SA 4.0")

![A monoshock with spring, preload collar, damper body and piggyback reservoir.](photo:r3d-rear_shock.png "Render: Transitions Lab, CC BY-SA 4.0")

Linkage bearings (needle roller and bushing) are greased at intervals of about 10,000–20,000 km on road bikes and after every few wet rides off-road; seized linkages make the rear harsh and wear the shock. Shock absorbers are checked for oil leakage at the shaft seal, a bent shaft, worn mounting bushes and fading damping (bouncing more than once after being pushed down). Most OEM shocks on small bikes are sealed and replaced as a unit, as the K-Light 125 manual directs for a bent rod or fatigued spring.

> [!WARNING] Gas-charged shocks
> Many rear shocks contain nitrogen at 1–2 MPa behind a floating piston or bladder. **Never** heat, puncture or dismantle a gas shock without first releasing the gas through the Schrader valve or following the maker's procedure, and dispose of unwanted shocks as directed.

## Job sheet 4.5.1: Measuring and setting sag

| Tools and materials | Reference values |
|---|---|
| Steel tape or sag tool, a helper, notepad, C-spanner or preload tool, centre or paddock stand | Rider sag road **25–33 % of travel**; static sag rear **5–15 mm** (road); manual travel figures |

1. Set tyre pressures and fill fuel to the usual level. Put the bike on the stand with the rear wheel off the ground. Measure vertically from the rear axle centre to a fixed mark on the subframe directly above it: **L1** (fully extended).
2. Take the bike off the stand, bounce the suspension, and let it settle; measure **L2a** after lifting the rear slightly and letting it settle down, then **L2b** after pushing it down and letting it rise. The true value lies between (stiction makes them differ). Static sag = L1 − (L2a + L2b)/2.
3. With the rider in normal riding position (feet on pegs, a helper steadying the bike), repeat the measurement to obtain L3. Rider sag = L1 − L3.
4. Compare with the target. To reduce sag increase preload; to increase sag reduce it. On cam-ring twin shocks, set both shocks to the same step.
5. Repeat for the front, measuring from the dust seal to the fork lower clamp or slider with the forks extended and then loaded. A cable tie around a fork tube pushed against the seal records maximum travel used after a ride.
6. Check static sag: if correct rider sag leaves almost no static sag, the spring is too soft; if static sag is excessive, too stiff. Recommend the correct spring rate rather than more preload.
7. Record the settings, adjuster positions and rider mass for future reference.

**Pass criteria:** rider and static sag within target at both ends with preload adjusters inside their range; front and rear sag balanced so the bike sits level; both twin shocks set identically.

# 4.6 Wheels and tyres

> [!OBJECTIVES]

## Wheels

**Spoked wheels** use a steel or aluminium rim laced to the hub with 32–40 steel spokes in tension, usually crossing (tangential lacing) so that drive and brake torque is carried by spokes pulling on the hub. They absorb impacts, can be repaired by replacing spokes and are standard on off-road machines, but they need a tube unless the rim is sealed (as with cross-spoke rims that anchor the spokes outside the air chamber). **Cast wheels** (aluminium alloy, sometimes magnesium) are stiff, run tubeless tyres and need no truing; they crack or bend rather than deform gracefully and cannot be repaired. The K-Light 125 uses aluminium alloy wheels, 1.85 × 17 front and 3.00 × 15 rear (rim width in inches × diameter).

![A cast alloy rear wheel with cush-drive hub and sprocket carrier.](photo:r3d-rear_wheel_cast.png "Render: Transitions Lab, CC BY-SA 4.0")

![Hubs and bearings: exploded front hub with axle, spacers, sealed bearings and seals, and the rear hub with cush drive and sprocket carrier.](fig:W02)

![Wheel types: 36-spoke wire wheel with drum hub, 21-inch motocross wheel with rim lock, and a cast alloy wheel with disc, plus a half-section through rim, tyre, bead and valve.](fig:W01)

**Runout** is measured with a dial gauge as the wheel turns on its own bearings or in a truing stand: **radial (vertical)** runout is up-and-down movement of the rim, **axial (lateral, horizontal)** runout side-to-side. The K-Light 125 manual gives standard **within 1.0 mm** and a limit of **2.0 mm** in both directions, plus a spindle bend limit of **0.2 mm**. Many manufacturers specify tighter limits for spoked rims, typically 0.5–1.0 mm radial and 0.5–0.8 mm axial in service.

### Truing a spoked wheel

Truing corrects runout by adjusting spoke nipples. Tightening a spoke pulls the rim towards the hub flange it comes from, and towards the hub radially. For lateral runout, tighten the spokes from the side opposite the high spot and loosen those from the same side by equal amounts, a quarter to half a turn at a time over three to five spokes either side of the high point. For radial runout, tighten spokes at the high spot and loosen those opposite. Work gradually round the wheel, checking the rim's offset relative to the hub (dish) with a ruler against the hub face, and finish with all spokes at similar tension (an even ring when tapped, or a tension meter). After truing, file any spoke ends that protrude through the nipples and fit a sound rim band before the tube; MAVCC Unit X job sheets cover respoking a damaged rim.

### Wheel bearings

Wheel bearings are sealed deep-groove ball bearings (K-Light 125: 6202-2RS front, 6303-2RS rear) separated by a spacer tube that is clamped by the axle. Check by turning the inner race with a finger (roughness, notchiness) and rocking it radially and axially; on the bike, lift the wheel and rock it at top and bottom with the axle tight. The K-Light 125 manual states that bearings removed **must not be reused**. Remove with a bearing puller or drift on the inner race through the spacer, after removing the oil seal; fit new bearings by pressing only on the **outer race** with a driver matched to its diameter, with the sealed side outwards, ensuring the spacer is in place before the second bearing goes in.

## Tyres

![Tyre: sidewall markings including size 110/70-17 54H, DOT date code and tread wear indicator position, and a section through a tyre showing bead, bead wire, carcass plies, belt, sidewall and tread.](fig:F20)

### Construction

- **Bias (cross-ply)**: two or more carcass plies of nylon or polyester cord laid at about 30–40° to the circumference, each ply crossing the previous one. The sidewall and tread work together, which makes the tyre stiff and resistant to damage, suited to heavy loads, small machines, off-road and cruisers, but it runs hotter at speed and the contact patch distorts in corners.
- **Bias-belted**: bias carcass with a belt of aramid or glass under the tread to stabilise it at speed.
- **Radial**: one or two carcass plies laid at close to 90° to the circumference, with a belt (often a **zero-degree** spiral-wound steel or aramid belt) under the tread. Sidewall and tread work independently, giving a flatter, more stable contact patch, lower heat build-up and lower weight. Radials need wider rims and are standard on sports and touring bikes above about 400 cc.

### Markings

A size of **110/70-17 54H** reads: section width 110 mm, aspect ratio 70 % (sidewall height 77 mm), "-" for bias construction (R for radial, B for bias-belted), 17 in rim diameter, load index 54 and speed symbol H. A **load index** of 54 means **212 kg** per tyre at the reference pressure; for comparison 49 is 185 kg, 59 is 243 kg and 73 is 365 kg. Speed symbols include **J 100, K 110, L 120, M 130, N 140, P 150, Q 160, R 170, S 180, T 190, H 210, V 240 and W 270 km/h**. Older sizes use inch markings such as 3.00-18 or 90/90-17 without a load and speed suffix on some commuter tyres.

The **DOT code** ends with four digits for week and year of manufacture: **2324** means week 23 of 2024. Rubber hardens with age even unused; most makers recommend inspection from five years old and replacement at **ten years** regardless of tread. "TL" means tubeless, "TT" tube type; a directional arrow shows rotation; a coloured dot often marks the light spot for alignment with the valve.

**Tread wear indicators (TWI)** are small raised blocks in the main grooves, located by a triangle or "TWI" mark on the shoulder; when the tread is level with them about **0.8–1 mm** remains. The K-Light 125 manual wear limit is **1 mm**, the legal minimum in the UK for machines over 50 cc and in many other countries; check national rules. Square-worn rear tyres from straight-line commuting make the bike drop suddenly into corners and should be replaced before the legal limit.

### Pressures

Set pressures cold, with an accurate gauge, to the manufacturer's figures: **190 ± 10 kPa (27.5 psi) front and 210 ± 10 kPa (30.5 psi) rear** on the K-Light 125, solo. Under-inflation makes the tyre flex and overheat, increases wear on the shoulders, and makes steering heavy and vague (the manual lists low pressure as a cause of heavy steering and instability); over-inflation shrinks the contact patch and reduces grip and comfort. Increase rear pressure with a passenger or heavy load when the handbook says so.

### Tube versus tubeless and puncture repair

A **tubeless tyre** seals on the bead and rim; a puncture usually deflates slowly because the rubber grips the object. A tube-type tyre can deflate suddenly when a tube splits. A tubeless repair must be a **combined plug-patch** fitted from inside after removing the tyre: the injury is cleaned and reamed, the plug-patch stem pulled through to fill the hole and the patch bonded over the inner liner with vulcanising solution. Most tyre makers permit repairs only in the central part of the tread, for injuries no larger than about **6 mm**, with no previous repair nearby and no sidewall or shoulder damage, and some limit subsequent speed or forbid repair of motorcycle tyres entirely. External string plugs are a **temporary** get-you-home measure only. A tube is repaired with a vulcanised patch and the tyre inspected inside for the cause; a tube must **never** be fitted to cure a leak in a tubeless tyre that is damaged.

### Balancing

A wheel and tyre assembly has a heavy spot; at speed the imbalance produces a vertical or lateral shake (often felt at 80–110 km/h in the bars). **Static balancing** on a stand with the wheel on its own bearings or a frictionless arbor lets the heavy spot settle at the bottom; weights are added opposite until the wheel stays where it is placed. Stick-on weights (5–10 g segments) go on the rim centre, spoke weights clip to spoke nipples. Narrow motorcycle wheels are usually balanced statically; dynamic (two-plane) balancing on a machine is used for wide rear tyres.

## Job sheet 4.6.1: Removing and fitting a tubeless tyre and balancing

| Tools and materials | Reference values |
|---|---|
| Manual or machine tyre changer, three tyre levers, rim protectors, valve core tool, tyre soap or lubricant, new valve, bead-breaker, balancing stand, wheel weights, pressure gauge, safety glasses | K-Light 125 tyres 90/90-17 front, 130/90-15 rear, tubeless; **190/210 kPa**; bead seating pressure per tyre maker, never above the maximum moulded on the sidewall |

1. Remove the wheel (support the bike securely; the K-Light 125 manual warns not to let the front wheel float above ground unsupported). Note the rotation arrow and protect the brake disc; **never** lay a wheel on its disc.
2. Remove the valve core and deflate fully. Break both beads with the bead breaker, keeping the breaker shoe clear of the rim flange and disc.
3. Push the bead opposite the valve into the rim's central well, lubricate, and lever the upper bead over the flange near the valve with two levers and rim protectors, working in small bites. Remove the second bead the same way.
4. Inspect the rim for dents, cracks and corrosion at the bead seat; replace the rubber valve (rubber valves age with the tyre). Inspect the inside of the old tyre to find the puncture cause.
5. Lubricate both beads of the new tyre, check its rotation arrow, and align the balance dot with the valve (the K-Light 125 manual marks rotation and valve position). Fit the lower bead, then the upper, using the well and small lever bites.
6. Inflate without the valve core until both beads pop onto their seats, checking that the moulded rim line is concentric on both sides; do not exceed the maker's seating pressure. Fit the core and set pressure.
7. Balance: place the wheel on the stand, let it settle and mark the lowest point. Attach trial weights opposite (at the top) and repeat until the wheel stays in any position. Clean the rim before bonding final weights.
8. Refit the wheel, tighten axle nuts to specification (K-Light 125 front 75–88 N·m, rear 100–113 N·m) and check brake operation.

**Pass criteria:** beads evenly seated with the rim line concentric, no leaks at valve or bead (soap test), wheel stays in any position on the balance stand, correct rotation direction, pressures and axle torques correct.

## Energy and friction

A brake converts kinetic energy into heat. The energy depends on the square of speed, so doubling speed quadruples the heat.

```eq
E = ½ × m × v²
ΔT = E_disc / (m_disc × c)
```

where m is the mass of machine and rider (kg), v the speed (m/s), m_disc the disc mass and c the specific heat capacity of the disc material (about 460 J/kg·K for steel).

Worked example: a K-Light 125 (145 kg curb mass) with an 80 kg rider, m = 225 kg, stops from 90 km/h (25 m/s).

```eq
E = 0.5 × 225 × 25² = 70,300 J ≈ 70 kJ
```

In a hard stop the front brake does about 70 % of the work. If 90 % of that heat enters a 0.9 kg steel disc before it can be shed, E_disc ≈ 0.7 × 0.9 × 70.3 kJ ≈ 44 kJ and ΔT = 44,000/(0.9 × 460) ≈ **106 K**. Repeated stops from high speed or a long descent therefore bring discs to 300–400 °C and push heat into calipers and fluid, which is why fluid boiling point and pad fade resistance matter.

## Drum brakes

A **drum brake** has two curved shoes with bonded linings mounted on a backplate, pivoted at one end on an anchor pin and pushed apart at the other by a flat **cam** turned by a lever, cable or rod; **return springs** pull them back. The drum is part of the hub. In a **single leading shoe (SLS)** brake, one shoe (the leading shoe) faces the drum rotation so friction drags it harder into the drum (self-servo), while the trailing shoe is pushed away, giving roughly two-thirds of the braking from one shoe. A **twin leading shoe (TLS)** brake has two cams, each operating one shoe at its leading end, linked by an adjustable rod; both shoes self-servo forward, giving much higher forward braking (and weaker reverse braking). TLS front drums were fitted to sporting machines until discs replaced them in the 1970s. Drums are enclosed, cheap and resist water and mud, but fade with heat because the drum expands away from the shoes. The rear brake of many commuters and scooters (Honda CG125, most 110 cc step-throughs) is still a drum.

MAVCC Unit XII lists the wear signs: a lip on the drum's open edge (the original diameter, showing the friction surface has worn), hairline heat cracks (the hub is weakened and must be replaced) and glazing (blue, burnished surface that can be sanded off). Drum internal diameter and lining thickness are checked against the manual, and the **wear indicator** on the cam lever shows when the shoes are near the limit.

## Hydraulic disc brakes

### Pascal's principle and force multiplication

![A floating disc gripped by a four-piston radial caliper, with pads visible in the caliper window.](photo:r3d-brake.png "Render: Transitions Lab, CC BY-SA 4.0")

![A floating front disc with drilled friction ring, dark carrier and riveted floating buttons, gripped by a two-piston sliding caliper with its mounting bolts, bleed nipple and banjo-fitted brake hose.](photo:render-brake.jpg "Render: Transitions Lab, CC BY-SA 4.0")

Pressure in a confined fluid is equal throughout, so a small master cylinder piston pushing on the fluid creates the same pressure on the much larger caliper pistons, multiplying force in the ratio of their areas. Lever leverage multiplies it again.

```eq
p = F_mc / A_mc
F_clamp = p × A_caliper
F_friction = 2 × μ × F_clamp
T_brake = F_friction × r_eff
```

where A is total piston area on one side of the disc, μ the pad friction coefficient (about 0.35–0.50) and r_eff the effective radius at the pad centres.

Worked example: an 11 mm master cylinder, a lever ratio of 4:1, a hand force of 100 N, a floating caliper with two 27 mm pistons, μ = 0.40 and r_eff = 120 mm on a 280 mm disc (as on the K-Light 125).

```eq
F_mc = 4 × 100 = 400 N
A_mc = π × 5.5² = 95.0 mm²
p = 400 / 95.0 = 4.21 N/mm² = 4.21 MPa
A_caliper = 2 × π × 13.5² = 1,145 mm²
F_clamp = 4.21 × 1,145 ≈ 4,820 N
F_friction = 2 × 0.40 × 4,820 ≈ 3,860 N
T_brake = 3,860 × 0.120 ≈ 463 N·m
```

The hydraulic ratio is 1,145/95.0 ≈ 12, and the total ratio from hand to clamp about 48:1. At the tyre (radius 0.297 m) this torque gives 463/0.297 ≈ 1,560 N of braking force, a deceleration of 1,560/225 ≈ **6.9 m/s² (0.71 g)** from the front brake alone. The price of multiplication is movement: the master cylinder must displace twelve times the fluid volume of the pad clearance taken up, so a larger master cylinder gives a harder, less powerful lever and a smaller one a softer, more powerful lever with more travel.

### Calipers and mounts

A **floating (sliding) caliper** has pistons on one side only; the caliper body slides on greased pins so that the reaction pulls the outer pad onto the disc. It is light and cheap and used on most small machines, including the K-Light 125. Seized slide pins cause uneven pad wear and drag. A **fixed (opposed-piston) caliper** has pistons on both sides of a rigid body (two, four or six pistons), giving more even pad pressure and higher stiffness. A **radial mount** bolts the caliper to the fork leg with bolts parallel to the disc's radius rather than perpendicular to it, so the caliper bracket is loaded in a stiffer direction and flex under braking is reduced. Discs are solid or drilled stainless steel, either fixed to the hub or **floating** on bobbins so they can expand radially without warping.

The piston **seal** is square-section in a groove; it distorts as the piston moves and pulls it back slightly when pressure is released (rollback), and as pads wear the piston slides further through it, so a disc brake adjusts itself.

### Pads

**Organic (resin) pads** use fibres and fillers in a resin binder; they are quiet, gentle on discs and bite well cold, but fade at high temperature and wear quickly. **Sintered pads** are metal powders fused under heat and pressure; they work well wet and hot and last longer, but wear discs faster and must only be used on discs approved for them. Semi-metallic and ceramic compounds lie in between.

> [!SPEC] K-Light 125 brakes (K-Light 125 manual)
> Front disc **280 mm**, thickness **4.0 mm new, 3.0 mm limit**; rear disc **240 mm**, **5.0 mm new, 4.0 mm limit**; disc runout limit **0.15 mm**. Pad friction material **6.0 mm new, 3 mm limit** front and rear. Fluid **DOT 4**. Lever free play **10–20 mm**, pedal **20–30 mm**. Caliper mounting bolts 22–29 N·m; disc bolts 10–12 N·m with thread lock; banjo (hollow) bolts **30 N·m** with new sealing washers; brake light on at about 15 mm lever travel.

### Brake fluid

Glycol-ether fluids (DOT 3, DOT 4, DOT 5.1) are **hygroscopic**: they absorb water from the air through seals and the reservoir vent, typically 1–3 % in a year or two, and the boiling point falls sharply. FMVSS 116 sets minimum boiling points:

| Fluid | Base | Dry boiling point | Wet boiling point (3.7 % water) | Compatibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DOT 3 | Glycol ether | 205 °C | 140 °C | Mixes with DOT 4, 5.1 |
| DOT 4 | Glycol ether, borate ester | 230 °C | 155 °C | Mixes with DOT 3, 5.1 |
| DOT 5.1 | Glycol ether, borate ester | 260 °C | 180 °C | Mixes with DOT 3, 4 |
| DOT 5 | Silicone | 260 °C | 180 °C | **Never** mix with glycol fluids |

Vapour bubbles from boiling fluid compress, so a hot brake with old fluid can go to the bar suddenly. Replace glycol fluid every **two years** (typical OEM interval). Glycol fluid strips paint, so cover painted parts. The K-Light 125 manual warns **never** to reuse fluid, use fluid from an opened container that has stood for a long time, mix brands or types, or change fluid in rain or strong wind, and to clean brake parts only with brake fluid, isopropyl alcohol or ethanol, since petrol and mineral oils swell the rubber seals.

## Combined braking (CBS)

A **combined braking system** applies both brakes from one control, so a rider who uses only the rear pedal (common among commuter riders) still gets front braking. The K-Light 125 uses a hydraulic CBS: the rear pedal operates a master cylinder that feeds the rear caliper and, through a **CBS pump** (a secondary master cylinder with its own reservoir, adjusting valve and plunger) and a hose, one piston circuit of the front caliper; the handlebar lever operates the front brake alone. The manual describes an internal leak test (screw in the adjusting bolt by hand while holding the pedal down: if it turns, the CBS pump leaks internally and is replaced) and an emergency setting: if the front caliper leaks, fully screwing in the CBS adjusting bolt lets the rear brake work alone. Pedal pushrod clearance is set to **0–0.2 mm** so the plunger is not pre-loaded. Mechanical CBS systems on scooters (Honda, Hero, TVS) use an equaliser link on the left lever that pulls both brake cables. Moto Guzzi fitted linked "integral" brakes from 1975 (850 T3); Honda's Dual-CBS links both levers to multiple caliper pistons on larger machines.

## ABS

An **anti-lock braking system** prevents wheel lock by modulating pressure. Each wheel has a **wheel speed sensor** (inductive or Hall-effect) reading a toothed **tone ring** (pulse ring) on the hub or disc carrier. The ECU compares wheel speeds and deceleration with the estimated vehicle speed; when a wheel's slip exceeds a threshold (roughly 10–30 %) the **hydraulic modulator** closes an inlet valve to hold pressure, opens an outlet valve to release fluid into a low-pressure accumulator, and a pump returns it to the master cylinder side, cycling several times per second until the wheel regains grip. The rider feels this as pulsing at the lever. BMW offered the first production motorcycle ABS on the K100 in 1988; modern units such as Bosch's ABS 10 weigh well under 1 kg. **Cornering ABS** adds an **inertial measurement unit (IMU)** that measures lean angle and pitch, so the ECU reduces brake pressure build-up and slip thresholds at lean and limits rear-wheel lift; Bosch Motorcycle Stability Control was first fitted on the 2013 KTM 1190 Adventure. Rear-lift mitigation reduces front pressure when the rear wheel decelerates faster than the front, indicating a stoppie.

ABS faults are read with a diagnostic tool; the most common causes of a warning lamp are a damaged tone ring, a sensor air gap out of specification (typically 0.4–1.2 mm), dirty sensors, mismatched tyre sizes and low battery voltage. Bleeding an ABS system may require the diagnostic tool to cycle the modulator valves.

## Regulation

In the European Union, Regulation (EU) No 168/2013 requires **ABS on L3e motorcycles above 125 cc** for new types from **1 January 2016** and for all new vehicles registered from **1 January 2017**; L3e-A1 motorcycles (up to 125 cc and 11 kW) must have **ABS or CBS**. In India, the Central Motor Vehicles Rules as amended in 2016 require **ABS on two-wheelers above 125 cc** and **CBS or ABS at or below 125 cc**, for new models from April 2018 and all new vehicles from **April 2019**. A June 2025 draft notification proposed ABS on all new L2 two-wheelers from January 2026, but manufacturers contested its scope and timing; check the current notified text before relying on it.

## Job sheet 4.7.1: Replacing pads and measuring disc thickness and runout

| Tools and materials | Reference values |
|---|---|
| Socket and Allen set, torque wrench, micrometer (0–25 mm), dial gauge and magnetic base, piston spreader or flat lever, brake cleaner, copper-free anti-squeal paste (backs of pads only), new pads and pins as required, gloves, safety glasses | K-Light 125: pad **6.0 mm new, 3 mm limit**; front disc **4.0/3.0 mm**, rear **5.0/4.0 mm**; runout **0.15 mm**; caliper bolts 22–29 N·m; disc bolts 10–12 N·m |

1. Cover painted parts. Remove the pad pin plug and loosen the pad pin while the caliper is still bolted on. Remove the caliper mounting bolts and lift the caliper off the disc.
2. Remove the pad pin, pads and pad spring. Measure friction material at the thinnest point; if either pad is at or below **3 mm** replace **both pads in the caliper together** (K-Light 125 manual). Uneven wear indicates seized slide pins or a sticking piston.
3. Clean the caliper with brake cleaner (no compressed air on brake dust). Check piston dust seals for splits and fluid leaks. Clean and regrease slide pins with silicone grease and renew their boots if damaged.
4. Push the piston back squarely with a spreader, watching the reservoir level; it will rise as fluid returns. On CBS machines check both reservoirs.
5. Measure the disc thickness with the micrometer at several points round the swept area; replace if below the limit (front **3.0 mm**, rear **4.0 mm**) or if deeply scored. With the wheel fitted and axle tight, mount a dial gauge on the fork leg with the stylus 5–10 mm inside the disc's outer edge and rotate the wheel; replace or remount the disc if runout exceeds **0.15 mm** (check first that wheel bearings are good and the disc seats are clean).
6. If replacing a disc, clean the hub seat, apply thread lock and tighten the bolts in a diagonal pattern to **10–12 N·m**.
7. Fit new pads with their spring, insert the pad pin, refit the caliper and tighten mounting bolts to **22–29 N·m**, then the pad pin.
8. Pump the lever or pedal until it is firm **before** moving the machine. Set the fluid level. Bed in the pads with a series of moderate stops from 50 km/h, avoiding heavy stops for the first 100–200 km.

**Pass criteria:** both pads above limit and evenly worn, discs within thickness and runout limits, slide pins free, all bolts at torque, lever or pedal firm on the first stroke after pumping, wheel turns without drag, brake light working.

## Job sheet 4.7.2: Bleeding a hydraulic brake

| Tools and materials | Reference values |
|---|---|
| Ring spanner for bleed nipple, clear tube, catch bottle, sealed new DOT 4 fluid (or as marked on the reservoir cap), vacuum bleeder (manual method), rags, gloves, safety glasses | Reservoir kept **at least one-third full** (K-Light 125 manual); bleed nipple torque per manual; banjo bolts 30 N·m with new washers |

1. Park upright, turn the handlebar so the reservoir is level, and cover painted parts. Remove the reservoir cap, plate and diaphragm.
2. Fit the clear tube over the bleed nipple and into the catch bottle with a little fresh fluid in it, so air cannot be drawn back.
3. Lever method: squeeze and hold the lever, open the nipple a quarter turn, close it before the lever reaches the bar, then release the lever. Repeat, topping up the reservoir so it **never** falls below one-third (MAVCC Unit XII: never allow the level to fall low enough to draw air in).
4. Vacuum method (K-Light 125 manual): apply the vacuum pump to the nipple, open it and draw fluid through while topping up the reservoir, until clear, bubble-free new fluid flows. Close the nipple, then pump the lever several times quickly.
5. Continue until the fluid in the tube is clear, the old darker fluid has been flushed, and no bubbles appear. On CBS systems bleed each circuit in the order the manual gives (front lever circuit, rear pedal circuit, CBS circuit).
6. If air persists at the high point of the system, tie the lever back overnight or tap the hose and caliper to dislodge bubbles; on ABS models use the diagnostic tool's bleed routine if specified.
7. Tighten the bleed nipple, refit the dust cap, set fluid to the upper mark, and refit the diaphragm and cap.
8. Test: the lever or pedal must be firm and not sink under steady pressure. MAVCC notes a spongy lever almost always indicates air.

**Pass criteria:** firm lever and pedal with free play to specification, no leaks at nipple or banjos, fluid level correct, waste fluid stored for approved disposal, brake light and (if fitted) ABS lamp self-check normal.

## Job sheet 4.7.3: Drum brake shoe inspection and adjustment

| Tools and materials | Reference values |
|---|---|
| Socket set, vernier caliper, drum gauge or internal caliper, emery cloth, high-temperature grease, brake cleaner, dust mask | Lining thickness, drum diameter limit and pedal free play from manual (typical lining limit 1.5–2.0 mm; pedal free play 20–30 mm) |

1. Check the wear indicator on the brake cam lever first: if the pointer is beyond the mark with the brake applied, the shoes or drum are worn out.
2. Remove the wheel and pull the backplate out of the drum. Wear a dust mask and wet-clean the dust; **never** blow it out with compressed air.
3. Measure lining thickness at the thinnest point and replace both shoes if at the limit, contaminated with oil or grease, or cracked. Replace the hub oil seal if grease contamination came from it.
4. Measure drum internal diameter in several directions; replace the hub if beyond the limit, out-of-round, or showing heat cracks. Remove light glazing with emery cloth (MAVCC Unit XII).
5. Remove the shoes by folding them together against spring tension. Clean the cam and anchor pin and apply a light film of high-temperature grease; check the cam for wear in its bush and the return springs for stretch.
6. Refit shoes and springs, check the cam lever's angle to the rod or cable (it should be close to 90° with the brake applied, which gives the most leverage), and refit the wheel and torque arm.
7. Adjust pedal or lever free play with the adjuster nut on the rod or cable to the specified range (rear pedal **20–30 mm** on many commuters). After any chain adjustment, recheck.
8. Spin the wheel to confirm there is no drag, and check that the brake light comes on before the brake starts to bite.

**Pass criteria:** linings and drum within limits, no contamination, cam lubricated and free, free play within specification, no drag, brake light operating correctly, and a confident stop on the road test.

## Pre-ride check: T-CLOCS

The Motorcycle Safety Foundation's **T-CLOCS** checklist covers what a rider should check before riding:

- **T, Tyres and wheels**: pressure (cold), tread depth and damage, spoke tension or cast-wheel cracks, bearings, brakes operating.
- **C, Controls**: levers and pedal free play and return, cables free with no kinks or fraying, throttle snapping shut in every bar position, hoses not cracked.
- **L, Lights and electrics**: headlight high and low beam, tail and brake light from both controls, indicators, horn, battery terminals secure.
- **O, Oil and other fluids**: engine oil level, brake and clutch fluid levels, coolant, fuel, and evidence of leaks underneath.
- **C, Chassis**: frame, suspension action and leaks, chain or belt tension and lubrication, fasteners.
- **S, Stands**: side and centre stands spring back fully and the side-stand switch cuts the engine when the stand is down in gear.

## Chassis tune-up

MAVCC Unit XVII sets out a chassis tune-up that complements the engine tune-up of Module 2: inspect and lubricate all control cables, adjust clutch, brake and throttle free play, check every fastener, inspect, lubricate and adjust chain and sprockets, check horn, lights and safety equipment, check wheels for damage and loose spokes, set tyre pressures, check fluid levels, aim the headlight, lubricate the chassis as the manual specifies, and finish with a wipe-down.

Cables are lubricated at their pivots and inner wire by a pressure luber or drip method (Teflon-lined cables need no oil), and must be routed away from hot surfaces and tight bends. When fitting a new cable, MAVCC recommends laying it alongside the old one before removal and removing the fuel tank if the cable passes under it, so the routing is correct. With the engine idling, turn the bars from lock to lock: idle speed must not rise, which would show a cable being pulled by the steering. Headlight aim is set on level ground with the rider seated, against a wall chart or beam setter, so the dipped beam's cut-off falls the specified amount (typically about 1 %) below the lamp centre height at the stated distance.

Detailing is the final step and the one the customer sees: MAVCC's advice includes not using silicone rubber dressing on seats, grips or tyres, since it makes them slippery, and keeping wax off matt finishes.

## Pre-delivery inspection

MAVCC Unit XVIII sets out uncrating, assembly and preparation of a new machine. Modern machines arrive more complete, but the pre-delivery inspection (PDI) is still the dealer's legal and safety responsibility, and the checklist below follows MAVCC's job sheet with current additions.

1. Unpack, check the frame number and engine number against the paperwork, and inspect for transit damage; record any damage before assembly.
2. Fit components shipped loose (handlebars, mirrors, front wheel, mudguard, screen) to the manual's torque values; route cables and hoses as shown in the assembly manual.
3. Fill and charge the battery if dry-charged, or test a factory-sealed battery for open-circuit voltage (above about 12.6 V for a fully charged 12 V lead-acid battery); connect **positive first, negative last**.
4. Check engine oil level, final-drive oil on shaft machines, coolant level and brake and clutch fluid levels; top up as required with the specified fluids.
5. Add fuel and check for leaks at the tank, pump, hoses and injector.
6. Set tyre pressures; check wheel nuts and axle torques and spoke tension.
7. Adjust clutch, throttle and brake free play, chain slack and alignment.
8. Check all lights, horn, indicators, instrument lamps, interlocks (side stand, clutch, neutral), kill switch and, where fitted, ABS lamp self-check.
9. Start and warm the engine; check idle speed, read the ECU for stored fault codes, and check for leaks.
10. Check fasteners across the machine, including fork clamps, steering stem nut, caliper bolts, swingarm pivot, shock mounts and footpeg brackets.
11. Road test: brakes (separately and, on CBS, the combined function), clutch, gear shifting through all gears, steering, suspension, speedometer, and absence of noises.
12. Remove transit protection and anti-corrosion wax from the exhaust (it smokes when first heated), clean the machine, fit the registration plate and record the PDI on the service record. Hand over the toolkit, manual and keys, and brief the customer on running-in and the first service at 300 km (K-Light 125).

> [!WARNING] Torque and road tests
> A PDI is complete only when every fastener the dealer touched has been tightened with a torque wrench to the manual's figure, and the machine has been road-tested in a safe place. Record the values; they are evidence if a fault is later disputed.

## Review questions

1. Why are service intervals given as distance or time, whichever comes first?
2. What do the K-Light 125 schedule codes I, A, C, R and L mean?
3. A K-Light 125 owner rides 800 km a month. When is the second oil change after the 300 km first service due, by distance and by time?
4. List the six letters of T-CLOCS and one check under each.
5. Why must idle speed be checked with the bars turned from lock to lock?
6. Why are new cables laid alongside the old ones before the old ones are removed?
7. In which order are battery cables connected and why?
8. What must be removed from a new exhaust before delivery, and why?
9. List five items you would road-test during a PDI.
10. Why must brake fluid be changed even on a machine that covers little distance?

## Answers

1. Fluids, rubber, and lubricants age and absorb moisture over time regardless of use, while wear depends on distance; whichever limit is reached first governs.
2. I inspect (and clean, adjust, lubricate or replace as necessary), A adjust, C clean, R replace, L lubricate.
3. Oil is replaced every 3,000 km; at 800 km per month the next change after 300 km is due at 3,300 km, about 3.75 months later, but the 3-month interval comes first, so the change is due at about 2,700 km, three months after the first service.
4. Tyres and wheels (pressure); Controls (free play); Lights and electrics (brake light); Oil and fluids (engine oil level); Chassis (chain slack); Stands (side stand returns and switch cuts the engine).
5. A cable routed too tight or trapped by the steering pulls the throttle open as the bars turn, raising idle; this is a dangerous fault.
6. So the new cable follows exactly the same route and guides, avoiding kinks, hot surfaces and points where steering movement would pull it.
7. Positive first, negative last (and negative off first on removal), so that a spanner touching the frame while the positive terminal is being tightened cannot short-circuit the battery.
8. Anti-corrosion wax or oil applied for transit; it smokes and smells when the exhaust heats up, alarming the customer, and can stain the finish.
9. Brakes (each control and the combined function), clutch release and take-up, gear changes through all gears, steering and straight-line stability, suspension action, instruments and speedometer, and absence of abnormal noises.
10. Glycol fluid absorbs water over time, lowering its boiling point and causing internal corrosion, regardless of distance travelled.

# Glossary

**ABS (anti-lock braking system)**: a system that measures wheel speeds and modulates brake pressure to prevent wheel lock.
**Aspect ratio**: tyre sidewall height as a percentage of section width.
**Assist clutch**: a clutch whose ramps add clamping force under drive torque so lighter springs can be used.
**Back-cut dog**: a dog whose driving face is undercut a few degrees so that torque holds the gears in engagement.
**Bias tyre**: a tyre whose carcass plies cross at an angle of about 30–40° to the circumference.
**Brinelling**: indentation of bearing races by balls or rollers under static load or shock.
**CBS (combined braking system)**: a system in which one control applies both front and rear brakes.
**Clutch basket**: the slotted outer housing of the clutch, driven by the primary gear.
**Constant-mesh gearbox**: a gearbox in which all gear pairs are always meshed and ratios are selected by locking gears to their shaft with dogs.
**Contra spring**: the spring that closes the driven pulley of a CVT.
**Countershaft**: the gearbox output shaft carrying the front sprocket.
**Cush drive**: rubber blocks between the rear sprocket carrier and hub that absorb drivetrain shock.
**CVT (continuously variable transmission)**: a transmission using variable-diameter pulleys and a belt to give a stepless ratio range.
**Damper-rod fork**: a telescopic fork that damps by forcing oil through fixed holes in a rod.
**Detent plate**: a notched plate on the shift drum that, with the stopper arm, holds each gear position.
**Dogs**: lugs on a gear face that engage slots or dogs on an adjacent gear to lock it to the shaft.
**DOT code**: tyre marking whose last four digits give week and year of manufacture.
**Drag (clutch)**: incomplete release of a clutch, causing creep and hard shifting.
**Fixed caliper**: a caliper with opposed pistons on both sides of the disc.
**Floating caliper**: a caliper with pistons on one side that slides on pins to clamp the disc.
**Fork offset**: perpendicular distance from the steering axis to the front wheel spindle.
**Hygroscopic**: absorbing moisture from the air, as glycol brake fluids do.
**IMU (inertial measurement unit)**: a sensor of accelerations and rotation rates used to calculate lean and pitch.
**Load index**: a number on a tyre corresponding to its maximum load at reference pressure.
**Mainshaft**: the gearbox input shaft that carries the clutch.
**Motion ratio**: ratio of wheel travel to shock travel in a rear suspension.
**Preload**: initial compression of a suspension spring at full extension.
**Primary drive**: the gear or chain drive from crankshaft to clutch.
**Quickshifter**: a system that briefly interrupts engine torque to allow clutchless gear changes.
**Radial tyre**: a tyre whose carcass cords run at about 90° to the circumference, with a belt under the tread.
**Rake**: angle of the steering axis from vertical.
**Rising rate**: a suspension characteristic in which wheel rate increases with travel.
**Runout**: deviation of a rotating part from true rotation, radial or axial.
**Sag**: suspension compression from full extension under the weight of the machine (static) or machine and rider (rider sag).
**Shift drum**: a grooved cylinder whose rotation moves the shift forks.
**Shim stack**: a set of thin steel discs covering damper piston ports that bend to control oil flow.
**Slipper clutch**: a clutch that slips under back-torque to prevent rear-wheel hop on downshifts.
**Speed symbol**: a letter on a tyre giving its maximum rated speed.
**Spring rate**: force needed to compress a spring by unit distance, in N/mm.
**Steering damper**: a hydraulic damper that resists rapid steering movement.
**Tone ring**: toothed ring read by an ABS wheel speed sensor.
**Trail**: ground distance from the steering axis intersection to the front contact patch centre.
**Twin leading shoe**: a drum brake with both shoes applied at their leading ends.
**Unsprung mass**: mass moving with the wheel and not supported by the springs.
**USD fork**: an upside-down fork with the large tube clamped in the yokes and the slim tube at the axle.
**Variator**: the drive pulley of a CVT whose rollers close it as engine speed rises.
**Wheelbase**: distance between the front and rear wheel spindles.
**X-ring chain**: a sealed chain using rings of X-shaped section between the plates.
